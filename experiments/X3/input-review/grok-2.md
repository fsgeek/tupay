I'll review the five draft inputs named in the brief and look only at those files.The five drafts disagree on what a legal quorum is, when a grant is allowed across a membership change, and when liveness is even required. Those three gaps would send the TLA+ author and the Go author down different designs. Smaller holes in expiry, decision records, and a few pairs would make faithful artifacts fail checks or pass for the wrong reason.

## 1. A grant under the old configuration stays legal after voters have already accepted the new one

**Where:** `contract/v3.md` terms “Committed” and “current configuration”; C3.2; C3.3; the `Agreed` proposal shape (“for a configuration, epoch, members and request id”); `observe/schema.md` v5 validity rule for a `Grant`; `tests/pairs-v4.md` P35, P36, and P46.

**Why it is a defect.** C3.2 excludes a voter from an old-epoch grant only if that voter has recorded `ConfigCommitted` for a higher epoch. Agreeing to the new configuration is not that record. `Agreed` is recorded only by the deciding node, so the voter’s promise is not in the trace at all. The schema’s check repeats the same predicate and adds “anywhere earlier in the trace,” but the schema also says there is no cross-process clock, so “earlier” is jsonl order. Joint configurations are declared not to be configurations and to have no epoch, so the usual joint-quorum grant cannot be represented. P46 still requires the first grant on a brand-new membership to outrank every old token, while the configuration proposal has nowhere to carry the highest granted token or `grantor_term`.

C3.2 also says the grant’s configuration is “the current one at the deciding node.” The term “current configuration” is the committed configuration with the highest epoch, a global fact. A deciding node that has not heard the commit is a different fact. The failure-model locus text says that node still holds authority under the old configuration in its own view. P35 and P36 then require something stronger than C3.2: a removed node, and a node that never learned the commit, take part in no old-configuration grant.

**Scenario.** Epoch 0 is `{n1,n2,n3}`. Token 1 has been granted. The operator asks for `{n4,n5,n6}`. `n1` and `n2` agree to epoch 1, and `n4` and `n5` agree, which is enough under C3.3. The proposal that was agreed names the epoch, the members, and the request id. Before anyone records `ConfigCommitted`, `n2` and `n3` agree to a grant of token 2 under epoch 0. `n2` has not recorded `ConfigCommitted`, so C3.2 and the schema’s part 1 both allow that grant. Epoch 1 is then committed. `n4` and `n5` know nothing of token 2. They grant token 2 again. Both grants are legal under the written C3.2 check, and C1.1 is already false. The same race with a one-node overlap is enough: the commit quorum can seal a high-water of 1 while another old majority that shares only a voter who already said yes to the new set issues token 2.

A second, independent break: `n2` records `ConfigCommitted` for epoch 1 after it sends its vote and before that vote is ordered in the jsonl. The schema then rejects a grant that was honest at send time. The deciding node cannot see that fact. Message fate was fixed when the vote was sent.

**Repair.** Make the safety rule the promise, not the commit record. A node that has agreed to epoch `e` or higher must not agree to a grant under a lower epoch, and must not agree to a different membership for an epoch it has already answered. That promise has to be durable across an ordinary restart. Check it on the voter’s own log at send time. Put the highest granted token and the highest `grantor_term` inside the configuration proposal and inside `ConfigCommitted`, and require every later grant under the new epoch to use a token strictly above what the commit carried. Say explicitly that “current” for C3.2 is the deciding node’s highest recorded epoch, and that a grant under an epoch below the globally highest commit is already a C3.2 failure once any counted voter had promised a higher epoch. Give epoch 0 and learner copies `decided: false`, and require exactly one `decided: true` per epoch greater than 0.

## 2. “Majority” and “re-added” have two different quorum rules

**Where:** `contract/v3.md` “Majority of a configuration”; `failure-model/v3.md` F3.3; `observe/schema.md` v5 part 1 (the `Grant` check and the `ConfigCommitted` check); `testbed/schedule-format.md` v5 Rule L3.

**Why it is a defect.** “More than half of its members, not counting amnesic members” is two quorum rules:

- Remove amnesic members from the set, then take a majority of who remains.
- Keep the original denominator, and refuse to count an amnesic vote toward it.

The schema’s grant check is the second rule: a majority of `members`, and no counted voter who is amnesic and not re-added. Its commit check never mentions amnesia, so a configuration commit is held to a weaker rule than a grant. F3.3 says a node is re-added only by a committed configuration whose **request was made after** the amnesia. The contract says a committed configuration has re-added the node **since** the amnesia, which includes a request that was already in flight when the disk was replaced.

Amnesia is also invisible to peers. `Fault` is an `env` record. The vote itself is visible only as `Agreed` at the decider, on receipt. A delayed vote sent before amnesia and a vote sent after it look the same to the leader. The schema nevertheless says the grant is invalid if a counted voter “is” amnesic. That predicate has no time and no sender event.

**Scenario.** Five members, two of them amnesic. The shrinking reading needs two votes from the other three. The schema needs three. A protocol built to the shrinking reading can commit and grant on a quorum that does not intersect an earlier majority of the full set: the earlier majority can have been `{n1,n4,n5}` with `n4` and `n5` now amnesic, and the new quorum `{n2,n3}`. Those two quorums do not intersect, so both can grant, and C1.1 fails while every local check passed.

Separately: `n2` sends a vote, then restarts amnesic, and the vote arrives afterward. The leader counts it. Part 1 fails the grant. No confirmation round closes the race, because a confirmation can be delayed across the same amnesia. The other direction is just as bad: a request that already listed `n2` was sent before the amnesia and commits after it. Under F3.3 `n2` still does not count. Under the contract it does. One author admits the vote; the other rejects it.

The parenthetical “none amnesic and not re-added” can also be read as “no voter is amnesic, and the voter was not re-added.” Under that reading every grant after a successful re-add fails part 1, including the run P45 describes.

**Repair.** One sentence: the threshold is more than half of the configured members, amnesic members stay in the denominator, and a vote counts only if the sender was eligible at send time. Eligible means the voter is not sitting in an amnesic incarnation, or a configuration that lists it was committed and its request was sent after that incarnation’s amnesia. Use one of those clocks, the request’s send or the commit, and delete the other. The voter must be able to attest eligibility in the vote (incarnation and highest epoch it still knows). The judge should follow that attestation plus the voter’s own log. Apply the same eligibility rule to configuration-commit quorums in part 1. State that a delayed vote sent before amnesia remains countable, and a vote sent by an amnesic incarnation is not.

## 3. Liveness is either vacuous or impossible once a reconfiguration is in flight

**Where:** `contract/v3.md` “Pending,” C1.4 under v3, C3.4; `failure-model/v3.md` “After the last fault” and F3.3; `testbed/schedule-format.md` v5 Rules R1, R3, and L3; `tests/pairs-v4.md` P39 and P52–P54.

**Why it is a defect.** Four clauses pull apart.

- The contract turns C1.4 off while a **reconfiguration request** is pending at a member of the majority. Schedule L3 turns it off while **a request** is pending. In this same document a `request` is a client grant request. Read that way, the premise is false for the entire interval in which a grant could be issued, so C1.4 obliges nothing, while P52–P54 still require a grant.
- Pending starts when a node records `ReconfigRequest` “or by learning of it from another node,” and it ends only when that node records **that request’s** commit or refusal. Only the receiver has a `ReconfigRequest` event. Learners have no event, and learning `ConfigCommitted` for a different membership is not defined to clear pending. After amnesia the only copy of the request is gone, but pending never ended, because the new incarnation recorded neither commit nor refusal.
- The contract’s C3.4 chain breaks if the receiver crashes: it did not “stay up” from the moment the request arrived. Schedule L3 starts the window again after the later of arrival and the last fault. Amnesia is a fault. After an amnesic restart the schedule again requires commit or refusal, and the receiver no longer has the request. The harness sends it once. F3.3 wiped every durable copy. The closed refusal list (`superseded`, `invalid`, `busy`) has no reason for “forgotten” or “no eligible majority.”
- `busy` refuses every later request at that receiver, and the earlier request is forbidden to be refused as `busy`. A request whose own C3.4 premise is false (a majority of the requested set is crashed or amnesic and cannot be counted) therefore sits forever. If learners also marked it pending, every majority contains a node with a request stuck in pending, and C1.4 never turns on again. A later healthy request to the same receiver is refused `busy`.

**Scenario.** `{n1,n2,n3}`, all up. The operator sends `r1`, naming three lease nodes of which two are already crashed for good, to `n1`. The set is non-empty and contains only lease nodes, so it is not `invalid`. Nothing else has committed, so it is not `superseded`. It cannot be `busy`. C3.4 does not require an answer, because no majority of the requested set is up. `n1` replicates “`r1` pending” to `n2` and `n3`. From here the contract no longer requires any client to obtain a grant. The operator sends a healthy `r2` to `n1`. `r2` is refused `busy`. P39’s shape is the other horn: the receiver records the request, crashes, and restarts amnesic before answering. Under the schedule window the request must still be committed or refused; under the contract the crash already cancelled the duty; in the process the request no longer exists.

**Repair.** Define pending only at the receiver that recorded `ReconfigRequest`, and end it on that receiver’s commit, that receiver’s refusal, amnesia of that receiver, or learning that a configuration with a higher epoch committed. Make schedule L3 say “reconfiguration request,” matching the contract. State that C3.4 applies to a request the receiver still has after the last fault; a copy destroyed by F3.3 creates no duty unless the schedule sends it again. Add one refusal reason that a receiver may record when no eligible majority of the current configuration or of the requested set remains up, so a stuck request cannot occupy `busy` forever.

## 4. Pause, clock steps, and “unexpired” describe three different holders

**Where:** `failure-model/v3.md` F1.3 and F3.2; holder locus; `contract/v3.md` Lifetime, holder, and the fencing paragraph; `testbed/schedule-format.md` v5 Rule R2; `tests/pairs-v4.md` P6, P44, and P50. Schedule alias `holder` (Rule A1) is a fourth definition.

**Why it is a defect.** F1.3 says a paused holder resumes and continues as if no time had passed. That is the case fencing exists for. F3.2 says the paused process’s clock keeps advancing through the pause, and Rule R2 repeats it. The contract says each process measures grant time on its own clock, and clocks are otherwise unrelated, so there is no shared expiry instant. P50 says a backward jump on the holder’s clock makes it believe an expired grant is still valid, so client belief does consult that clock. P6 says the paused holder resumes and writes. After a pause that outlasts `L` in run time, a client that believes its clock will decline to write, and a client that follows F1.3’s “no time had passed” will write. The failure model’s holder is whoever **believes** the grant is unexpired. The contract’s holder **holds** an unexpired grant. The schedule’s `holder` alias is whoever received the latest `Grant`, expired or not.

**Scenario.** `c1` receives a grant of lifetime `L`. It is paused for longer than `L`. During the pause its clock advances with run time, and the schedule also allows a clock step on the paused client. On resume one implementation treats the grant as expired and will not send the write P6 is about. Another ignores the clock because F1.3 told it no time had passed, writes, and then fails P50’s theory of belief. A third compares the grantor’s expiry timestamp with the client’s clock; those clocks are unrelated, so the comparison is arbitrary. Around the same expiry, P44 jumps the grantor’s clock and the holder’s clock in opposite directions. Nothing says which clock decides whether a new grant may be issued.

**Repair.** Split the two timers. A pause freezes the paused process’s grant timer, and on resume the client still presents the token it holds; say that in F3.2 as an exception, and delete “as if no time had passed” or make it that exception. A clock **step** is the only fault that moves a process’s clock relative to run time. Expiry is that process’s own clock minus the local time at which it recorded receipt, compared with duration `L`. The grant carries a duration, not an absolute time. Storage continues to ignore clocks. Point the `holder` alias at the same definition the pairs use, or say the alias ignores expiry.

## 5. The C3.5 checker ignores the inputs that actually decide, and compares sets as lists

**Where:** `contract/v3.md` C3.5 and “Decision inputs”; `observe/schema.md` v5 “Decision records” and part 2; mutation paragraph for P31–P54 in `tests/pairs-v4.md`.

**Why it is a defect.** Part 2 requires equal outcomes whenever `inputs` are equal as name-value sets, **refs ignored**, across every run of the side. The same addendum says a grant or a configuration decision includes the `Agreed` records it counted **by ref**. Refs are then ignored, so two grants that cite different votes, different clients, and different tokens have equal inputs whenever the copied name-value pairs match. Grant outcomes are defined as token and client only: `config_epoch`, `lifetime`, and `grantor_term` may diverge freely. `ConfigCommitted` members are a set in the contract and an array in the record; `[n1,n2,n4]` and `[n4,n1,n2]` are different JSON values and would be unequal outcomes.

The other reading makes the check blind. If the author copies the chosen token into a name-value input, two runs whose tokens differ have unequal inputs, and part 2 does not fire. The mutation for C3.5 says an unrecorded clock or random draw produces **equal** inputs and **different** tokens. That happens only when the token is absent from the compared values. The required `Agreed` proposal already contains the token. Authors cannot satisfy the mutation text and the input list with the same record.

**Scenario.** Two runs each grant under epoch 0 with members `{n1,n2,n3}`. One grant goes to `c1` with token 1, the other to `c2` with token 2. Both input lists are `{members, config_epoch}` plus refs to different `Agreed` records. Part 2 reports `UNEXPLAINED inputs` against every correct service. An author who adds `token` and `client` as values to stop the false failure then passes part 2 even when the token was chosen from an unrecorded clock, which is the mutation that was supposed to fail.

**Repair.** Define equality on the transitive closure of refs, with set-equality for `members` and a canonical encoding for numbers. Grant outcomes should include `config_epoch` and `grantor_term`. State that fields the decision chose, such as the token, are outcomes and are compared, and that embedding them in `inputs` does not remove them from the outcome. Keep the rule that an input which was consulted and which changes the outcome must appear as a compared value: the clock reading, the candidate request set, the previous high-water token.

## 6. P51 requires storage to reject a holder as soon as some other grant exists

**Where:** `tests/pairs-v4.md` P51; `contract/v3.md` fencing paragraph and C1.2.

**Why it is a defect.** The fencing paragraph says a newer grant does not by itself stop an older holder. Storage rejects the old token only after a write or a read with a newer token has been accepted or served. P49 and P50 include that newer write. P51 does not. It says: minority amnesia, a new grant, the previous holder’s write is rejected. An implementation of C1.2 still accepts the old write while the ledger is unchanged. An implementation that rejects it as soon as a `Grant` exists has to give storage information the storage events do not carry, and it violates the fencing paragraph. P5’s older phrase “after a new grant is accepted” is the same ambiguity; P51 copies it into the v3 pairs.

**Scenario.** Ledger is 4 after `c1`’s write. A minority member restarts amnesic. A new grant gives `c2` token 5. `c2` has not touched storage. `c1` writes token 4 again. C1.2 accepts it. P51 says it is rejected.

**Repair.** Word P51 like P49: the new holder writes (or reads) and that call is accepted or served; only then is the previous holder’s write rejected.

## 7. Restart identity and several new event fields cannot be filled from the rules given

**Where:** `observe/schema.md` record identity `(proc, seq)`, v5 `ConfigCommitted` / `decided` / causes, F3.1 versus `Fault`; `contract/v3.md` event list for `ReconfigRefused` and `ReconfigAck`; `failure-model/v3.md` F3.3; `testbed/schedule-format.md` v5 triggers and Rule R1.

**Why it is a defect.** `causes`, `answers`, and input `ref`s identify records by `(proc, seq)`. An amnesic node has no durable state, so it cannot continue its counter, and the schema gives it no incarnation. After restart, `(n2, 1)` is two records. The contract requires every initial member’s first record to be `ConfigCommitted` for epoch 0, and the schema requires `decided: true` only on the node that decided. Epoch 0 has no deciding node. If every member sets `decided: true`, every run starts with several commits of epoch 0 and P33 is already failed. If none do, epoch 0 has no decision record.

`ReconfigRefused` always has an `epoch`. The text defines that field only for `superseded`. For `invalid` and `busy` the refusing node has nothing to put there, and neither does `ReconfigAck`. `causes` on `ReconfigAck` and on a follower’s `ConfigCommitted` are optional, “if the message carried” a `(proc, seq)`. C3.3 is then true or false in the trace depending on whether the author felt like copying an id. Schema v5 says an F3.1 step is `ReconfigSend` and not a `Fault`. The same addendum’s trigger sentence, and the v1 rule that every schedule step emits `Fault`, both still speak as if the new actions emit `Fault`. One trace has a `Fault` for a reconfiguration and the other does not, which moves “the last fault” if a tool treats every `Fault` as a fault. The failure model says a reconfiguration request is not a fault.

Rule R1 adds the selector `non-member` and says it is resolved as Rule A1. `non-grantor` has an explore-all / lowest-number rule. `non-member` does not, and “the current configuration” used to resolve it is the same global-versus-local hole as in defect 1.

**Scenario.** `n2` emits `ConfigCommitted` epoch 0 at seq 1, later amnesically restarts, and emits seq 1 again. A `Grant` ref to `(n2, 1)` matches both. Separately, three initial nodes each emit epoch 0 with `decided: true` before any request. Part 1 and C3.1 have no unique decision to check. A third run: the operator is told `refused` / `busy` and the ack’s `epoch` is 0 in the model and null in the implementation.

**Repair.** Identity is `(proc, incarnation, seq)`. Incarnation starts at 0 and increases by one on every amnesic restart; it does not need disk. Epoch-0 records are `decided: false` except one deployment record, or they are all `decided: false` and C3.1 starts at epoch 1. `epoch` on a refusal is null unless the reason is `superseded`. The reply that justifies `ReconfigAck` always carries the receiver’s commit or refusal identity, and `causes` always names it. F3.1 emits `ReconfigSend` only. Give `non-member` the same resolution rule as `non-grantor`, against the latest `decided: true` configuration, or against epoch 0 before any commit.

## 8. Smaller holes that will still split a pair of careful authors

**Invalid requests are not requests.** The new term says a reconfiguration request carries a set of lease nodes. C3.4 and Rule R1 say an empty set, or a set naming a non-lease process, is a legal request refused as `invalid`. An author who enforces the term drops the message and records nothing. The operator then waits forever, and C3.4 never starts. Repair: the payload is a request id plus a set of process names, which may be empty or may name a non-lease process; `invalid` is decided and recorded by the receiver.

**Success is keyed by membership, not by request id.** C3.3 allows the operator to be told success once “the configuration it requested” is committed. Two requests with the same members and different ids can both be acked `committed` after one `ConfigCommitted`, whose `request_id` names only one of them. The other author refuses the second as `superseded`. Repair: outcome `committed` is legal only when `request_id` on the ack equals `request_id` on the unique `decided: true` record.

**`grantor_term` is required on writes and reads and absent from the contract’s event signatures.** The term says it is carried with the token on every write and read. The restated signatures for `WriteAttempt`, `WriteAccept`, `WriteReject`, and the four read events omit it. Schema v1 records it. One author will omit it from the Go structs derived from the contract list. Repair: put `grantor_term` in those eight signatures in `contract/v3.md`.

**Positive integers have no width.** Tokens and terms are positive integers with no upper bound. A TLA+ model will use unbounded numbers. A Go implementation that uses `int64` wraps and then violates C1.1. Repair: state that token and term arithmetic does not wrap, and that exceeding the implementation’s range is a recorded failure rather than a new grant.

These four change edge behavior and record layout. They do not change the main quorum design. They sit below 1–7 for that reason.

---

The schedule most likely to break artifacts built from these inputs is the following. A small initial configuration grants client A and A’s write is accepted. A is then cut off from every lease node and left able to reach storage. The operator delivers two reconfiguration requests, to two different members, before either is answered; the first request drops every current member and names only nodes that have never been members, and the second names a different non-empty set. A majority of the old configuration agrees to the first new membership, and before any of those voters records the commit, the old configuration grants client B a token, counting a voter that has already agreed to the new membership. The new membership then commits, the old grantor crashes and stays down, and one old voter that held the highest token restarts amnesic. The new members, storage, and a fresh client remain mutually connected; the new leader’s clock jumps backward, and that client is granted and writes. A’s clock jumps backward as well, and A writes with the original token.
